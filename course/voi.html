Value of information

The price of knowing

A new medicine looks worthwhile on average, but the evidence is thin. Fund it now and you may be wrong; fund a trial first and you spend money and time to be less wrong. Value-of-information analysis puts a price on that uncertainty, so research budgets can be judged with the same yardstick as treatments.

Three quantities

EVPI: the most knowing could be worth

If the truth were revealed for free, you would never fund a medicine that turns out to be poor value, or turn down one that turns out to be good. The expected value of perfect information is the expected net benefit of deciding with the truth, minus the net benefit of deciding now. Scaled to everyone the decision affects, it caps what any research could be worth.

EVSI and ENBS: what a real trial is worth

A trial with n participants narrows the uncertainty without removing it. Its expected value of sample information rises with n and levels off below EVPI. Subtract the trial's cost to get the expected net benefit of sampling. The best trial size maximises it, and if it is negative at every size, decide now.

When information is worthless

If the decision would not change whatever a study found, the study has no decision value, however interesting it is. Move the expected gain far from the break-even point and watch EVPI fall towards zero.

In the game

Registry studies in Health of Nations narrow a medicine's evidence bracket for a price. They are worth buying when the bracket straddles your funding line and the therapy is large enough to matter. That is this chapter's rule in a minister's uniform.
