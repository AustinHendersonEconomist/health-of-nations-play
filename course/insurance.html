Arrow and Pauly

Insurance and moral hazard

Insurance protects people from financial risk, which is worth a lot to risk-averse people. But insurance also lowers the price of care at the point of use, so people use more care than they would if they paid in full. The best coinsurance rate balances those two forces.

The RAND Health Insurance Experiment found about −0.2.
Setup: income NZ$60,000. If ill, care costs NZ$20,000 at full price. Demand responds to the out-of-pocket price with constant elasticity. Premiums are actuarially fair. Welfare is measured as a certainty equivalent in dollars.

What to notice

With no demand response, full insurance is best

Set the elasticity to zero. Insurance then does not change behaviour, so the optimum is 0% coinsurance: Arrow's 1963 result.

The deadweight loss is a triangle

When insured people pay only a share of the cost, they buy care that is worth less to them than it costs (Pauly 1968). The loss grows roughly with the square of the price cut.

Risk aversion pushes towards more cover

Raise risk aversion and the optimal coinsurance falls: protection becomes worth more than the waste it causes.

Beyond the model

Not all extra care is waste. If illness itself makes care more valuable, or people under-use effective care because of liquidity constraints, the triangle overstates the loss (Nyman 1999).

Readings: Arrow KJ. Uncertainty and the welfare economics of medical care. AER 1963;53(5):941–973. Pauly MV. The economics of moral hazard. AER 1968;58(3):531–537. Manning WG, et al. Health insurance and the demand for medical care: evidence from a randomized experiment. AER 1987;77(3):251–277.
