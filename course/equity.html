Distributional cost-effectiveness analysis

Who gains the QALYs?

Standard cost-effectiveness analysis treats a QALY as a QALY, whoever gains it. Distributional cost-effectiveness analysis (DCEA) asks how gains and opportunity costs fall across social groups, and lets the decision-maker state how much they care about inequality. With enough inequality aversion, a programme with fewer QALYs can be the better choice.

0 means a QALY is a QALY. Higher values give more weight to gains for groups with less health. Estimates for England put typical public views around 10–11.
Groups are deprivation quintiles of 1 million people each, with illustrative health-adjusted life expectancy (HALE) at birth from 62 to 72 years. Programme gains are added to each group's average. Opportunity costs are displaced evenly, as a simplifying assumption.

The equally distributed equivalent

hEDE = ( mean of hi1−ε )1/(1−ε)

The equally distributed equivalent is the level of health which, if everyone had it, would be valued the same as the actual unequal distribution. It equals average health when ε = 0 and falls towards the worst-off group's health as ε grows.

Where the threshold fits

Opportunity costs matter too. If displaced services would have benefited deprived groups more, a programme can widen inequality even when its own gains are evenly spread. Full DCEA models both sides.
