Every cost–benefit analysis of a long-lived policy applies one number that does more work than any other: the discount rate. It decides whether a life saved in 2100 counts for almost as much as a life saved today, or for almost nothing.

Where the number comes from

The Ramsey rule

Frank Ramsey's 1928 formula splits the social discount rate into two reasons to value the future less. One is pure impatience. The other is that future people are expected to be richer, so an extra dollar matters less to them.

Discounting the welfare of future people simply because they live later. Many philosophers set it at zero; a small positive value is often defended as the risk that humanity does not survive.
How fast an extra dollar loses value as people get richer. η = 1 means a dollar is worth half as much to someone twice as rich.
If the future will be poorer (g < 0), the rule can recommend a negative rate.
r =δ+η × g=3.5%

When you are unsure of the rate

Uncertainty makes the rate fall

Martin Weitzman showed that if we are unsure which rate is right, we should average the discount factors, not the rates. Far in the future the low-rate scenarios dominate that average, so the certainty-equivalent rate declines towards the lowest plausible rate. This is why the UK Treasury's schedule steps down over time.
