Discrete choice experiments

Choose, and be measured

A discrete choice experiment never asks how much you would pay. It asks you to choose between options that differ in several attributes, then works out your trade-offs from the pattern of your choices. Make ten choices below and the page estimates your own willingness to pay with a conditional logit model, the same way a published study would.

Your estimates

Make your choices first. Results appear after task 10. The orange tick marks the average of a simulated class of 80 students.

How it works

Random utility

Each option's utility is a weighted sum of its attributes plus a random term: U = βwait·wait + βcost·cost + βtravel·travel + βsuccess·success + ε. With extreme-value errors, the probability of choosing A is a logistic function of the utility difference. The weights are estimated by maximum likelihood.

From weights to dollars

Willingness to pay for an attribute is its weight divided by the negative of the cost weight, the marginal rate of substitution between that attribute and money. Ten choices give imprecise estimates, so the page adds a small ridge penalty. Real studies pool hundreds of respondents.

Designing good questions

The ten pairs were chosen so that no option dominates another and every attribute varies. This page uses a fixed design; studies use statistically efficient designs, often Bayesian, generated with pilot data.

What can go wrong

Some respondents ignore attributes or always choose the cheaper option. That is lexicographic behaviour, and it breaks the trade-off model. Test yourself: if you always chose the cheaper option, your cost weight becomes very large.

Readings: Lancsar E, Louviere J. Conducting discrete choice experiments to inform healthcare decision making. PharmacoEconomics 2008;26(8):661–677. McFadden D. Conditional logit analysis of qualitative choice behavior, in Zarembka (ed.), Frontiers in Econometrics, 1974.
