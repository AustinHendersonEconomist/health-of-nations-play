The demand for health

Health as capital

Michael Grossman's 1972 model treats health as a stock that people inherit, that wears out with age, and that they rebuild by investing medical care and their own time. People demand medical care because they want health, not because they want care. In the pure-investment version shown here, health is valued for the healthy time it produces.

MEC(Ht) = r + δt

Each year the person holds the health stock at which the marginal efficiency of health capital (MEC), the return on one more unit of health in healthy days earned, equals the user cost of capital. The user cost is the interest rate plus the depreciation rate. As depreciation rises with age, the optimal stock falls. When it would have to fall below a minimum, death occurs.

Higher wages raise the return on healthy days.
In Grossman's model, education makes a person a more efficient producer of health, which lowers the cost of investment.

Three predictions to test against data

Health falls with age, but spending on care rises

Rising depreciation lowers the optimal stock, yet replacing the faster-wearing capital takes more investment. Move the "rate at which depreciation rises" slider and watch the two lines diverge.

Education improves health

More efficient producers hold more health and live longer. This is one of the model's best-supported predictions, though how much of the education–health correlation is causal is still debated.

Higher wages raise the demand for health

Healthy time is worth more to higher earners, so the optimal stock rises with the wage.

A known weakness

Real people with poor health use more care, while the simple model links high investment with high health. Later work adds uncertainty and adjustment costs to reconcile the two.

Reading: Grossman M. On the concept of health capital and the demand for health. Journal of Political Economy 1972;80(2):223–255. The model here is a stylised pure-investment version with MEC(H) = A·w·e·H−γ, γ = 0.5, and an illustrative minimum stock.
